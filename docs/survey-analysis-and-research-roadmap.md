**Project:** MethodAlign IS — Agile versus Traditional Comparative Engine  
**Research level:** Undergraduate  
**Analysis date:** 27 July 2026  
**Source:** `docs/google_form_responses/MIS Survey  (Responses) - Form Responses 1.csv`

**Duplicate-screened analysis copy:** `docs/google_form_responses/MIS Survey (Responses) - duplicate-screened n107.csv`. The added `Source response row` column preserves traceability to the raw file.

## 1. Executive conclusion

The survey provides useful exploratory evidence that requirement uncertainty, flexibility, stakeholder involvement, documentation, security, risk, integration complexity, budget constraints, deadlines, and project size are associated with the methodology respondents report using.

It does **not yet validate the application's decision rule**. The survey measured the methodology that was used, not the methodology that should have been used. It also included Hybrid as an outcome, while the application must recommend only Agile or Traditional. Project success has a strong ceiling effect, and the sample is heavily confounded by industry.

The next research phase should therefore be **instrument refinement and independent decision-model validation**, not immediate adjustment of weights until the model fits these 111 records. The recommended sequence is:

1. document transparent data cleaning and retain a sensitivity analysis;
2. reformulate the application's questions as neutral project facts;
3. split the current broad constructs into conceptually clearer constructs;
4. obtain independent Agile/Traditional suitability labels from domain experts;
5. pilot the revised instrument;
6. freeze the scoring rule before evaluating it on unseen cases;
7. evaluate reliability, agreement, usability, and system performance in Chapter 6.

### Implementation decision recorded 27 July 2026

The project subsequently adopted a pragmatic 12-item Version 2 for client handover: four adaptation/readiness items, three delivery-constraint items, and five assurance/dependency items. A follow-up rule version uses provisional survey effect-size weights of 1.37, 0.64, and 0.99, weighted-range normalization, no strictness override, and reference centroids derived from the duplicate-screened Agile and Traditional cases. Same-sample binary agreement changes from 73/81 (90.1%) under equal weights to 74/81 (91.4%) under effect-size weights, but both remain calibration diagnostics against historical methodology used—not validated predictive accuracy. The five-construct/15-item design below is retained as the more extensive design alternative considered during instrument development.

Reproducible outputs are stored in `docs/research_analysis/` and generated by `scripts/analyze_survey.py`.

## 3. Recommended research framing

### 3.1 Main research question

> How can project characteristics be operationalized in an explainable decision-support application that recommends an Agile or Traditional methodology for information systems projects?

Possible sub-questions:

1. Which project characteristics are associated with the use and suitability of Agile or Traditional methodologies?
2. How reliably does the revised questionnaire measure those characteristics?
3. To what extent do the application's recommendations agree with independent expert judgments?
4. How usable and understandable is the resulting decision-support application?

### 3.2 Research aim

To design, implement, and evaluate an explainable decision-support application that uses project characteristics to recommend an Agile or Traditional methodology for information systems projects.

### 3.3 Research objectives

1. **To identify** project characteristics relevant to Agile and Traditional methodology selection through literature and practitioner evidence.
2. **To analyze** the relationships between project characteristics, methodology use, and perceived project success in the collected survey.
3. **To design and implement** a versioned, explainable decision model and application questionnaire for binary Agile/Traditional recommendations.
4. **To evaluate** the questionnaire's reliability, the decision engine's agreement with independent expert judgments, and the application's usability and functional correctness.

### 3.4 Suitable research design

A **pragmatic, mixed-method, design-science case study** is defensible for this project:

- quantitative survey analysis identifies patterns and supports instrument refinement;
- qualitative open-text analysis identifies factors omitted by the closed questions;
- design and implementation produce the research artefact;
- expert review and scenario/case evaluation test decision validity;
- user testing evaluates usability and explanation quality.

The final thesis should only use labels such as “mixed-method” and “design science” after they are justified with appropriate methodological literature.

Because the survey did not capture a participant identifier, completion duration, or attention-check item, careless responses cannot be identified conclusively.

## 6. Quantitative results

All project-factor scales were coded so larger values indicate more of the named characteristic. Requirement change was coded from 1 = Never/Rarely to 5 = Constantly; documentation from 1 = Minimal to 5 = Extensive; and size from 1 = Small to 4 = Very Large.

### 6.1 Project characteristics by methodology used

| Project characteristic | Overall mean | Agile mean | Hybrid mean | Traditional mean | Eta-squared |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Requirements change | 3.22 | 4.02 | 2.92 | 1.89 | 0.682 |
| Stakeholder involvement | 3.42 | 3.93 | 3.15 | 2.67 | 0.337 |
| Strict deadline importance | 3.68 | 3.22 | 4.00 | 4.30 | 0.222 |
| Budget importance | 3.92 | 3.44 | 4.19 | 4.59 | 0.299 |
| Project risk | 3.37 | 2.91 | 3.38 | 4.30 | 0.399 |
| Integration complexity | 3.46 | 3.11 | 3.46 | 4.15 | 0.253 |
| Security requirements | 3.63 | 3.04 | 3.73 | 4.70 | 0.416 |
| Documentation need | 3.36 | 2.61 | 3.58 | 4.63 | 0.542 |
| Project size (1–4) | 2.06 | 1.56 | 2.23 | 2.89 | 0.415 |
| Need for flexibility | 3.37 | 4.22 | 3.19 | 1.85 | 0.651 |
| Perceived success | 4.04 | 4.13 | 3.96 | 3.93 | 0.034 |

Interpretation:

- The strongest differentiators are requirements change, flexibility need, and documentation need.
- Agile projects report high change, high flexibility, and greater stakeholder involvement.
- Traditional projects report high documentation, security, risk, project size, budget constraints, deadlines, and integration complexity.
- Hybrid projects generally lie between the two profiles, which supports treating Hybrid as a delivery pattern rather than statistical noise.
- All ten project-characteristic differences are strong in this sample. However, their strength is probably inflated by industry composition and response-pattern clustering.

### 6.2 Perceived project success

The overall success mean is 4.04 out of 5. Scores of 4 or 5 account for 97 of 107 responses (**90.7%**); no respondent selected 1. This ceiling effect provides too little variation to test whether one methodology produces better outcomes.

| Methodology | n | Mean success | Percentage scoring 4 or 5 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Agile | 54 | 4.13 | 96.3% |
| Hybrid | 26 | 3.96 | 84.6% |
| Traditional | 27 | 3.93 | 85.2% |

The methodology groups explain only about 3.4% of success-score variance. In the raw-data permutation analysis, the group difference was not statistically persuasive (`p ≈ .17`), and bootstrap confidence intervals for pairwise mean differences crossed zero. The defensible conclusion is:

> The survey found differences in methodology context, but it did not establish a significant difference in perceived project success between Agile, Hybrid, and Traditional projects.

### 6.3 Reliability findings

| Proposed scale | Items | Cronbach's alpha |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Adaptive context | Requirement change, stakeholder involvement, flexibility need | 0.909 |
| Performance/constraint | Deadline, budget | 0.864 |
| Current Strictness proxy | Security, integration | 0.750 |
| Expanded assurance/complexity | Risk, integration, security, documentation | 0.918 |
| Agile belief items | Three Agile statements | 0.931 |
| Traditional belief items | Three Traditional statements | 0.936 |

These figures suggest high internal consistency, but they must be interpreted cautiously:

- alpha is partly inflated by the very strong Agile-to-Traditional separation in the sample;
- a high alpha does not prove that items measure one valid construct;
- the two-item Strictness scale is only marginally suitable and combines security with integration, which are conceptually different;
- within the Hybrid subgroup, the two-item Strictness alpha falls to approximately 0.41, suggesting instability;
- factor validity and measurement invariance have not been demonstrated.

The current application term **PERFORMANCE** should be renamed. Deadline rigidity and budget rigidity are constraints or predictability requirements; they are not observations of project performance.

## 8. Data-quality and validity warnings

### 8.1 Temporal instability

The first 15 submissions and later 96 submissions behave very differently. In the first 15, the adaptive-context alpha is about 0.49 and adaptive/control scores are slightly positively correlated. In rows 16–111, adaptive-context alpha rises to about 0.95 and adaptive/control scores correlate about `-0.81`.

Seventy-seven responses were submitted between 22 and 25 June. Collection waves may reflect different recruitment channels or populations. The thesis should identify how each wave was recruited and report wave-based sensitivity analysis. Time clustering alone is not a valid exclusion criterion.

### 8.2 Common-method and consistency bias

Respondents first state which methodology was used and then rate explicit statements praising Agile or Traditional. They may answer later items consistently with the method they already selected. The attitude scales therefore measure methodology belief or retrospective justification, not independent project suitability.

### 8.3 Outcome validity

“Which methodology was used?” is an observed historical choice. It is not a ground-truth answer to “Which methodology should be used?” Historical choices may have been imposed by contracts, management, procurement, or organizational habit—the open answers explicitly say this.

### 8.4 Sampling and generalizability

The sample is non-random, dominated by IT/Software and respondents with five years or less experience. Industry and methodology are strongly confounded. Findings should be stated as evidence from the sampled respondents, not universal facts about all information systems projects.

### 8.5 Ethical/data-management reporting

The thesis should document informed consent, participant eligibility, anonymity/confidentiality, storage, retention, withdrawal process, and whether any personally identifiable information was collected. The research dataset should not contain names or company names unless essential and ethically approved.

## 9. Evaluation of the current application model

### 9.1 Survey-to-application mapping

| Current application item | Closest survey evidence | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q11 Requirements likely to change | Requirement-change frequency | Good conceptual match. |
| Q12 Team can adapt quickly | Need for flexibility | Not equivalent: capability and need are different concepts. |
| Q13 Stakeholders available frequently | Stakeholder involvement | Related, but actual involvement does not prove future availability or decision authority. |
| Q14 Timeline adherence critical | Deadline importance | Reasonable match, but “critical” needs an anchored meaning. |
| Q15 Budget fixed | Budget importance | Not equivalent: importance is not the same as a fixed funding ceiling. |
| Q16 Security/compliance standards | Security level | Compliance was not directly measured. |
| Q17 Third-party integrations | Integration complexity | Related, but integration complexity can exist without third parties. |

Because Q12, Q15, Q16, and Q17 lack exact survey equivalents, any replay of the current engine against the Google Forms data is illustrative rather than a formal validation.

### 9.2 Illustrative replay result

Using the closest available proxy mapping, excluding probable duplicate pairs and excluding Hybrid cases from the binary comparison:

- binary cases: 81;
- agreement with methodology used: 68/81 = **84.0%**;
- approximate 95% Wilson interval: **74.5%–90.4%**;
- Cohen's kappa: approximately **0.68**;
- Agile recall: 41/54 = **75.9%**;
- Traditional recall: 27/27 = **100%**;
- 13 Agile cases were labeled Traditional;
- no Traditional cases were labeled Agile;
- 21 of 26 Hybrid cases were forced to Traditional and 5 to Agile.

This result indicates a **Traditional/conservative bias**, not proven decision accuracy. It is also unstable across collection waves: agreement is 2/9 among the first 15 respondents' binary cases but 66/72 in the later cleaned wave. It must not be presented as the final model accuracy.

### 9.3 Problems in the present research metrics

Several dashboard metrics are useful operational descriptions but are not independent validation:

- correlating a construct with an Agile/Traditional score derived from that construct is mathematically circular;
- comparing engine-recommended groups on the constructs used to create those groups is also circular;
- the seven “dependent variables” are calculated from the same three construct scores and are not observed outcomes;
- confidence based only on score distance is a decision margin, not statistical confidence;
- the strictness override is an expert rule that has not yet been independently validated.

For the thesis, label these as **internal diagnostic metrics**. Add independent criteria such as expert recommendation, actual project outcomes, usability scores, and explanation-understanding scores.

## 10. Alternative expanded questionnaire proposal (not adopted for Version 2)

### 10.1 Design principles

The scoring questionnaire should:

- ask neutral questions about the project, not whether Agile or Traditional is “better”;
- separate project need from organizational capability;
- use specific, symmetric anchors rather than generic agreement labels where possible;
- avoid double-barrelled items such as “security or compliance”;
- separate governance/assurance from technical integration;
- retain a binary recommendation but add implementation overlays when both sides are high;
- be pilot-tested and versioned before becoming the active questionnaire.

### 10.2 Proposed scored instrument

All items use a 1–5 scale. Each endpoint should be visible in the application.

#### A. Adaptation need — generally supports Agile

| ID | Question | 1 anchor | 5 anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | How much are the project's requirements expected to change after development begins? | Stable and fully agreed | Frequent, substantial change expected |
| A2 | How much uncertainty exists about the final solution and its acceptance criteria before development begins? | Fully defined and agreed | Substantial discovery is still required |
| A3 | How important is early user feedback for discovering whether the correct solution is being built? | Low importance | Essential throughout delivery |

#### B. Iterative-delivery readiness — generally supports Agile

| ID | Question | 1 anchor | 5 anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R1 | How often can an authorized business representative review work and make timely priority decisions? | Only at major milestones | At least weekly or whenever needed |
| R2 | How capable is the team of planning, building, testing, and reviewing work in short cycles? | No relevant experience/capability | Highly experienced and capable |
| R3 | To what extent can the solution be delivered and validated in useful increments? | Only usable when fully completed | Easily divided into valuable increments |

#### C. Predictive delivery constraints — generally supports Traditional

| ID | Question | 1 anchor | 5 anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | To what extent is scope fixed by a contract, procurement decision, or formal approval before delivery starts? | Scope can be reprioritized | Scope is contractually/formally fixed |
| P2 | How fixed is the delivery date due to law, contract, public launch, or external dependency? | Date can change | Date is externally immovable |
| P3 | How much formal approval is required to trade scope, budget, or schedule during delivery? | Team can make trade-offs | Multiple formal approvals required |

#### D. Assurance and governance — generally supports Traditional controls

| ID | Question | 1 anchor | 5 anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| G1 | What level of regulatory, legal, or audit obligation applies to the project? | None/minimal | Extensive mandatory obligations |
| G2 | What would be the consequence of a security, privacy, safety, or operational failure? | Low and easily reversible | Severe, harmful, or legally significant |
| G3 | How much formal documentation and end-to-end traceability is required for approval or audit? | Lightweight working notes | Complete controlled traceability required |

#### E. Dependency and coordination complexity — generally supports planning/control

| ID | Question | 1 anchor | 5 anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | How dependent is delivery on external vendors, legacy systems, or interfaces outside the team's control? | Mostly self-contained | Many critical external dependencies |
| D2 | How much coordination is required across teams, locations, or organizations for a release? | One small co-located team | Many distributed organizations/teams |
| D3 | How strongly must work follow a fixed sequence because later work depends on earlier completion or approval? | Work can proceed independently | Work has tightly coupled sequential dependencies |

This produces 15 scored items across five interpretable constructs. If questionnaire length must be reduced, retain at least three items per construct used for reliability claims; otherwise explicitly treat two-item groups as indices rather than robust latent scales.

### 10.3 Profile and validation fields

Collect separately from scoring:

- respondent role;
- years of relevant experience;
- industry;
- project type;
- team size;
- project duration;
- lifecycle stage;
- methodology experience;
- whether the respondent had decision authority;
- whether they are describing a completed, ongoing, or hypothetical project.

For research evaluation, additionally collect:

- methodology used;
- who selected it and whether it was imposed;
- an independently judged suitable methodology;
- outcome measures with factual anchors, such as schedule variance, budget variance, accepted-scope completion, defect severity, stakeholder satisfaction, and compliance findings.

Name, company name, and project name should not affect scoring. If retained operationally, keep them outside the research dataset or replace them with anonymous identifiers.

### 10.4 Questions to remove from scoring

The six statements “Agile is suitable...”, “Agile improves...”, and “Traditional is...” should not be application decision inputs. They reveal the expected answer and measure respondent attitude. They may remain in a separate research section for studying perceptions, preferably placed after all neutral project questions.

### 10.5 Recommendation logic

Do not immediately fit 15 independent weights to this small sample. First calculate five construct scores. Ask three to five domain experts to label project cases independently, then calibrate a compact and explainable model against consensus labels.

The application must still output Agile or Traditional, but it can attach a governance overlay:

- **Agile with enhanced governance controls** when adaptation/readiness and assurance are both high;
- **Traditional with iterative validation practices** when predictive constraints are high but discovery/user feedback is also important.

These remain implementation suggestions, not a third Hybrid recommendation class.

The hard Strictness override should be replaced or justified through expert validation. Security, compliance, and integration create control requirements, but they do not by themselves prove that iterative delivery is unsuitable.

## 11. Validation plan for the revised model

### Stage 1 — Content validation

Ask at least three relevant experts—such as project managers, business analysts, governance/security professionals, or academic supervisors—to rate each question for relevance, clarity, and construct fit. Revise ambiguous items and record the decision trail.

### Stage 2 — Cognitive pre-test

Conduct 5–8 short think-aloud sessions. Ask participants what each item means, how they choose a rating, and whether the anchors match real project situations.

### Stage 3 — Pilot study

Pilot the revised questionnaire with approximately 30–50 eligible respondents. Examine missingness, completion time, floor/ceiling effects, inter-item correlations, item-total correlations, and preliminary alpha/omega. Do not tune the final decision thresholds solely on this pilot.

### Stage 4 — Expert-labelled decision cases

Build a set of real anonymized projects or carefully designed project vignettes. Each case should be independently labeled Agile or Traditional by multiple experts. Report inter-rater agreement and create a consensus label before model calibration.

### Stage 5 — Freeze and test

Freeze questionnaire version and scoring-rule version. Evaluate on unseen cases. With limited undergraduate data, repeated stratified cross-validation may be reported as preliminary evidence, but a genuinely held-out or later external sample is preferable.

Report at minimum:

- confusion matrix;
- accuracy with 95% confidence interval;
- balanced accuracy;
- Agile and Traditional precision/recall;
- Cohen's kappa;
- calibration or decision-margin distribution;
- close-call/manual-review rate;
- error analysis by industry, experience, and project type.

### Stage 6 — Usability and explanation evaluation

Ask representative users to complete scenarios and evaluate task completion, time, errors, perceived usefulness, explanation clarity, and trust. A standardized usability scale may be used if properly cited and administered.

## 13. Claims that are safe and unsafe in the thesis

### Defensible claims

- In this sample, project characteristics differed strongly across the methodologies respondents reported using.
- Requirement change and flexibility showed the strongest Agile-oriented separation, while documentation, security, risk, and size showed strong Traditional-oriented separation.
- Hybrid cases usually occupied intermediate profiles.
- The survey did not demonstrate that one methodology was more successful.
- The current engine shows preliminary agreement with historical methodology use but still requires independent expert validation.

### Claims to avoid

- “The survey proves Agile is better.”
- “The model is 84% accurate at choosing the correct methodology.”
- “High security always requires Waterfall.”
- “Cronbach's alpha proves the questionnaire is valid.”
- “The calculated dependent variables prove project outcomes.”
- “These findings generalize to all information systems projects.”

## 14. Immediate decision

Do not delete all unusual-looking responses. Preserve the raw dataset, use the 107-response duplicate-screened dataset for the primary exploratory analysis, and show that the 111-response sensitivity analysis leads to the same substantive conclusions. The deployed effect-size-weighted calibration must be described as provisional and frozen for prospective testing; do not tune it further on these same 107 records. Next, validate the neutral questionnaire and obtain an independent Agile/Traditional criterion label.
